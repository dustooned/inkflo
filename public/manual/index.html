Making comics with inkflo

inkflo is a comic studio that runs in your browser. It follows the order professionals have used for a century: write it, plan the page, draw it, letter it, and get it ready to print or post. Each step has its own desk.

Comics as a craft grew up in newspapers and then in comic book studios of the 1930s and 1940s, where the work was split between a writer, a penciller, an inker, a colourist and a letterer. Many artists now do every job themselves, but the order of the steps stuck because each one builds on the last. inkflo keeps those steps as desks so you can work like a whole studio, alone or as a team.

The five desks

  • Script
  • Layout
  • Draw
  • Caption
  • Print

The tabs along the bottom of every page switch desks. Each tab shows that step's status for the page: to do, in progress or done. Mark a step done with the check button in the header. You can move back and forth freely: change the script after drawing, or recut panels after lettering.

Books, pages and saving

The home screen is your shelf. New book starts a one-page book, New vertical comic starts a tall phone-width episode, and Open starter comic makes your own copy of The Last Train, a two-panel example to explore and change.

Open a book to see its pages. Add pages, start a page from a template, reorder them, and use Continue to jump back to where you left off. Assets holds images the whole book can reuse, such as logos, reference photos and page-break art.

Bins keep those images sorted and one move away. In Draw, switch the sidebar from Layers to Bins. Tap an image to place it in the selected panel, or drag it onto the page and it lands in the panel you drop it on, centred where you let go. With a finger, hold an image for a moment, then drag; a quick swipe still scrolls the bin, and hold-and-lift opens its menu (place as reference, move to a bin, rename, Artist mark, remove). Drag an image onto a bin's name to move it there. + makes a bin; tap the open bin's name again to rename, reorder or delete it (its images go to Unsorted, nothing is lost). Drop image files from your computer onto the bins to add them to the open bin, or straight onto the page to add and place them at once.

Balloon bins (Caption → sidebar → Bins): six starter balloons, plus any balloon you keep. Select a balloon on the page and tap + Save to keep it with its shape, style, tails, speaker and words (joined balloons come along). Drag one onto the page to drop a copy there, or tap it for the middle of the page. Saved balloons belong to the book and travel in its .inkflo file; the copy you place isn't linked to the script.

Layout bins (Layout → sidebar → Bins): the built-in grids and your saved layouts. Tap one to lay out the whole page. Drag one onto a panel to split just that panel with it: drop Four panels on a wide bottom panel and it becomes a 2 × 2 grid inside it. + Save keeps this page's layout in the open bin. Saved layouts are kept on this device, so they're there in every book (the same list as page templates).

inkflo saves as you work, on this device, in the browser. Nothing is uploaded. To move a book to another computer or keep a backup, use Export → Book file (.inkflo), and Import .inkflo on the home screen to open it again. An .inkflo file keeps everything editable: every stroke, cut, balloon and script line.

Comic strips and your Artist mark

Comic strip on the home screen makes a strip in two clicks: 3 panels or 4 panels side by side (a 13 × 4 in newspaper daily), or a Stacked strip (4 × 12 in, three panels in a column that reads well on a phone and still prints). It opens in Draw with the panels cut; change the cuts in Layout and the size in Print whenever you like.

Your Artist mark is a signature, monogram or symbol you place on your work. In Draw, choose Artist mark and import a transparent PNG or WebP (or pick one of the book's images, or use Use as Artist mark in Assets). Place Artist mark puts it in the bottom-right corner inside the safe area; drag to move it, drag its corner to resize, snap it to any corner, or Hide it, which keeps it editable but leaves it out of the page and every export. The image is stored once in the book; copy it to another book with Assets → Copy assets.

To share a strip, use Export → Export files → Web image (PNG): pick the page and a width (Phone 1080, Web 1600, Large 2400 or your own) and see the exact pixel size before downloading. Print it with the Print shop or print-on-demand PDF, which follows the strip's trim.

Newspaper strips grew up in the 1890s and early 1900s, and their shape came from the page: a daily strip had to fit a narrow band across a newspaper, so three or four panels became the standard rhythm of setup, development and punchline. Cartoonists have always signed their strips, often working the signature into the art of the last panel. Today the same strips are posted to phones, where a stacked strip reads naturally as you scroll.
Open the starter comic, change one line of dialogue in Script, and watch Caption offer to update the balloon. It's the quickest way to see how the desks talk to each other.

ScriptWriting the page

A comic script describes each panel and lists its words. In inkflo each panel is a beat: a scene description, then the dialogue, one line per balloon.

  • Start a line with a name and a colon to name the speaker: MINA: Hold that door!
  • CAPTION: makes a narration box instead of a speech balloon.
  • + Add panel beat adds a panel; the arrows reorder beats.
  • Dialogue & panels assigns each line to a panel and a lettering preset, such as a whisper style for one character.
  • Send to Layout carries the beat count over, so Layout knows how many panels the page wants.

Writing the script as code

Prefer typing to cards? Switch Script to Code in the tool bar and write the whole page as plain text, like a screenplay. A legend beside the editor shows the syntax, colours mark each part as you type, and the panels update live (the Live list shows what inkflo has read). Switch back to Cards any time; both edit the same script.

There are two classic ways to write comics. In a full script, the writer describes every panel and every line before anyone draws, as in a film script. In the Marvel method, made famous at Marvel Comics in the 1960s, the writer handed the artist a plot; the artist designed the pages; and the dialogue was written to fit the finished art. inkflo supports both. Write everything first, or draw first and come back to Script to add the words. Caption will pick up the changes.
Keep balloons short. Lettering takes up space, and a panel crowded with words leaves no room for the picture. A common rule of thumb is about 25 words per balloon and a few balloons per panel.

LayoutPanels, gutters and the page

Layout turns a blank page into panels. Panels are made by cutting: every cut splits the page or a panel in two, and the space a cut leaves is the gutter. The cuts are the page; the panels are worked out from them, so you can always move, curve or remove a cut later.

The gutter is where comics happen. The cartoonist and theorist Scott McCloud called it closure (in Understanding Comics, 1993): the reader's mind fills in what happens between two panels. A narrow gutter reads as a quick moment; a wide gutter, or a long walk across the page, reads as time passing. Will Eisner, who named the medium sequential art in 1985, treated the panel border itself as a storytelling tool.

The Knife and Auto Shape

Choose the Knife (K) and draw a line through the page.

  • Across page cuts every panel the line crosses, from page edge to page edge.
  • One panel cuts only the panel you draw in.
  • Draw anywhere: from the board around the page, or just across the part you want split. A line drawn inside the page runs on to the edges by itself.
  • Hold Shift for a perfectly straight cut that turns in 15° steps. While you cut, a readout by the pen shows the angle in degrees with an arc from level; straight cuts click to level, upright and 45° when you get within a few degrees, and turn green with a guide across the page.
  • Auto Shape (on by default): pause at the end of a cut without lifting, and inkflo snaps it to the cleanest shape it sees: a straight line, an arc, a zig-zag for jagged action edges, or a smooth curve with the wobble taken out. Keep holding and drag to adjust the end, then lift.
  • Curve in the tool bar rounds every corner of the cut; set it to 0 for sharp zig-zags.
  • Uncut (E) removes a cut: click its gutter.

Grids, presets and rhythm

Presets (Splash, 2 rows, 3 tiers, 4, 6, 9) give you a classic grid in one click. Page templates stores and reuses layouts you like. The modular grid shows guides that the Knife snaps to, so panels line up across a book. Even out spaces a straight grid evenly.

The splash page, a single huge panel, has opened stories since the early superhero comics. The nine-panel grid is famous from Watchmen (Alan Moore and Dave Gibbons, 1986–87), where an unchanging grid acts like a metronome, so breaking it hits hard. Many cartoonists teach the grid as a rhythm: panel after panel like beats in a bar, with the last panel on the page (the downbeat) saved for the punchline or the turn.

Use Mark last panel as downbeat to flag the panel that carries the page's hit. Use Spread in the header to see facing pages together. Readers take in a whole spread at once, so a big reveal is best placed after a page turn, on a left-hand page, rather than where the reader's eye can catch it early.

For game makers: both Script's and Layout's code views have Copy JSON and Download: every panel's shape (in inches and as 0–1 page fractions), its position, any move or turn, its description and its lines with speaker and kind. Script also exports the whole book. Use it for cutscenes, visual novels, motion comics or your own reader.

Game engine package: Export → Export files → Game engine package (.zip) packs a page range for Unity, Godot, Unreal or Ren’Py at 1024, 2048 or 4096 px wide. Inside: manifest.json (pages, panels, camera boxes and balloons with speaker, text, size and tail points, all in pixels), each panel as a PNG cut to its shape, each page’s art without lettering, the lettering alone on transparent, the finished page, the dialogue as Yarn Spinner and/or Ink tagged by panel, and a README with loading examples. Lines come from Script; a panel with no script uses its balloons.

Moving and shaping panels

  • Resize gutters (R): drag a gutter or a panel's handle to move the cut under it.
  • Transform panel (V): move, rotate or scale a whole panel, for tilted panels and panels that burst out of the grid. Its art moves with it, and Draw keeps working inside it.
  • Curve edges (B): bend a gutter with handles.
  • Gutter, Border, Round corners and Gutter background set the look of every gutter on the page. With Border at 0 and a transparent gutter you get borderless panels, where art can run right up to its neighbour.

DrawPencils, inks and colour

Draw follows the traditional path from rough to finished: pencil the drawing loosely, ink the final lines on top, then colour underneath. Each panel has its own group of layers, so your marks stay inside the panel you start in.

Professional pencillers often drew in non-photo blue pencil, a light blue that the old camera-based printing process couldn't see, so only the black ink reached print. Inkers used brushes and dip pens; in manga the springy G-pen nib became the standard tool for lines that swell and taper with pressure. inkflo's pencil layer plays the role of non-photo blue: it shows while you work and stays out of print exports.

Layers and groups

Open Layers (tool bar or sidebar). Every panel has a group; there are also Background, Over panels (art that breaks out over the frames) and Gutters (art that lives in the gaps between panels).

  • Draw on: Panel under pen sends each stroke to the panel where you start it. Selected layer draws into one chosen layer anywhere.
  • The eye shows or hides a layer, the lock protects it, and the panel icon masks a group to its panel shape.
  • Adjustments changes a layer's hue, saturation, brightness or blur.

Colour and fills

The Colour tool (F) fills areas enclosed by your ink lines, on a colour layer under the ink, so the lines stay crisp.

  • Bucket: tap an area. Close gaps treats small breaks in your lines as closed. Fills stop at panel edges even where your lines don't.
  • Drag fill, Enclose and fill (lasso a region), Fill leftovers (catch the specks a fill missed) and Auto flats (fill every closed area of a panel with flat colours at once).
  • Check gaps (C) previews where a fill would leak before you commit.
Flats are a comic colourist's first pass: every shape filled with a plain colour so it can be selected and shaded later. Before computers, colourists painted colour guides by hand and engravers separated them into printing plates. The old four-colour comic look came from printing cyan, magenta, yellow and black in tiny dots.

Screentone and comic lines

Screentone (T) lays an editable dot or line pattern over a panel or a lassoed area; set dot shape, density (LPI), angle and colour. Comic lines (L) draws speed lines or focus lines as real ink strokes.

Printed dot patterns are older than comic books: Benjamin Day patented his Ben-Day dots process in 1879, and it gave old newspaper and comic book colour its dotty texture (later celebrated in pop art). Artists also pressed adhesive tone sheets, such as Zip-A-Tone, onto their pages. Manga made screentone an art of its own, cutting and scraping tone sheets for shading, skies and mood. Speed lines and focus lines are another manga and cartooning staple for motion and emphasis.

Selecting and transforming

  • Select lines (M) selects vector lines to move, rotate, flip, resize or duplicate.
  • Transform (R) lifts painted pixels to move, scale, rotate or flip them. Choose the selection shape: rectangle, ellipse, freehand, polygon (click the first point or press Enter to close) or magnetic. Keep original makes a copy.
  • Position image places, moves and resizes imported pictures (see Collage).
  • All of these work on panels you have moved or rotated in Layout.

Outlines

Open a layer's options in Layers (the ⋯ on its row) and choose an Outline: White, Black or any colour, with a width up to a quarter inch. The outline wraps everything on the layer (ink, colour, a pasted photo or a collage piece), so it works like a border effect in other comic apps.

  • A white outline lifts a character or a sound effect off a busy background so it reads at a glance.
  • A black outline "inks" a pasted photo or 3D render so it sits with your line art.
  • The outline prints and exports exactly as it looks. It pauses while you are drawing a stroke on that layer and comes back when you lift.
Outlining is an old cartoonist's trick for clarity: a thin white gap or a heavy black contour around the main figure separates it from the background, much as a stage light separates an actor from the set. Inkers often thickened outer contours and kept inner lines thin for the same reason.

DrawCollage and zines

inkflo can paste up a page from pictures, the way zine makers have with scissors and glue. Collage pieces stay non-destructive: the original image is always kept whole.

  1. Place image (sidebar) or paste a picture from another app with Ctrl/⌘+V.
  2. With Position image, drag to move and drag the corner to resize.
  3. Cut out piece: draw a loop around the part you want to keep.
  4. Torn edge roughens the cut like ripped paper; Paper edge adds a border of any colour, like a photo cut out with a white margin.
  5. Duplicate piece makes another copy to cut differently; Reset cut brings back the whole image.
Zines are small self-published magazines. Science-fiction fans made fanzines from the 1930s; punk bands and their fans made photocopied zines in the 1970s; the riot grrrl movement used them in the 1990s; and mini-comics and zine fairs carry on today. Cut-and-paste collage itself goes back to artists such as Picasso and Braque gluing paper into paintings in 1912, and to Hannah Höch's Dada photomontages. The look of a zine (ragged edges, mixed type, photos next to drawings) came from the tools: scissors, glue sticks and the photocopier.
Make a one-sheet mini-zine: set Print kind to Mini-zine in the Print desk, make 8 pages, collage them, and print Mini-zine (one sheet). The PDF comes with folding steps.

CaptionLettering

Caption places the words from your script onto the art. Click in a panel with Letter to place its next line, or use Place from script to letter the whole page. Drag a balloon to move it; drag the yellow dot to aim its tail at the speaker; the + beside a balloon adds a joined balloon, one underneath, or another tail.

For most of comics history, lettering was done by hand, in ink, often by specialist letterers working with a ruled guide (the Ames Lettering Guide was a favourite) to keep letter heights even. American comics settled on all-capital letters, partly because they stayed legible on cheap, absorbent newsprint. Digital comic fonts now do the job, but letterers still decide balloon placement, reading order and sound effect design. That's a craft of its own.

The style sheet

Style sheet sets the book's lettering sizes (Small, Body, Big, in printed points), the house font, and when lettering counts as too small. A live preview shows them at print size. Select a balloon and choose Save as character style to give a character their own font and balloon, then every new line by that character follows it. Rename a speaker there to rename them through the script and balloons at once.

Reading order and readability

Readers follow balloons left to right and top to bottom (right to left in manga). The inspector warns when balloons would be read out of script order, and when lettering is too small to read at print size or on a phone. Keep readable nudges too-small lettering back up. Review script changes shows balloons whose script line has changed, so you can update the wording without losing placement.

Place the first balloon of a panel near its top left, and keep tails short and pointing at mouths. Balloons should never cover a face or the key action.

CaptionType frames: text-led pages

Not every illustrated book is a comic. The Type tool (X) in the Caption desk sets text on the page, for illustrated novels, picture books, poems, short stories told beside a single image, and title or chapter pages.

  1. Choose Type and drag a frame on the page (or click for a ready-sized one, or right-click → Type frame here).
  2. Write in the Text box. Each line break starts a new paragraph; an empty line adds space.
  3. Pick a style to start from: Novel body, Chapter opening, Title, Poem, Pull quote or Picture-book.
  4. Drag the frame to move it and its corner to resize. A red + means more text than fits: enlarge the frame, add a column or reduce the size.

Text pages have their own long craft. Medieval manuscripts opened chapters with large painted initials, the ancestor of the drop cap. Printers settled on justified columns and first-line indents for continuous reading, while poems keep their line breaks and space between stanzas. Illustrated books, from Victorian novels with engraved plates to picture books where words and pictures share every spread, mix text and image in ways comics borrowed and changed. Type frames let you design pages in that tradition alongside your comic pages.
For a single-image story, place one large drawing or collage and add a Pull quote frame over a quiet area, with a background colour and a little inset. Then a Novel body frame below for the story.

Type frames print and export with the lettering, including the lettering separation, and they are kept in the .inkflo book with every setting.

PrintGetting ready to print

Choose a Print kind: US comic book, trade paperback, manga digest, European album, zine, picture book, webtoon and more. Each sets the trim size, bleed, safe area, binding and the resolution to draw at.

  • Trim is where the page is cut. Bleed is extra art beyond the trim, so a cut that wanders slightly never leaves a white edge. Keep words and faces inside the safe area.
  • Saddle stitch (stapled through the fold, like most comic books and zines) needs a page count in multiples of 4; perfect binding needs an even count. inkflo adds blank pages where they're needed and tells you.
  • Preflight checks every page: resolution, lettering size and position, ink coverage.
A printed comic is folded from large sheets, so pages come in groups (signatures) of 4, 8 or 16. That's why a stapled comic book has a page count divisible by 4, and why a single folded sheet can hold an 8-page mini-zine.

Two-colour printing on a risograph, a stencil duplicator from Japan, became a favourite of zine makers and small presses because it's cheap, fast and gives bold, slightly imperfect colour. Designing in two inks is a classic small-press skill.

Vertical scroll comics

Vertical scroll stacks pages into one long episode for phones: numbered image strips, a ready-to-upload index.html, and an episode.json. Set the space between pages and the background. Page-break art draws an image of your choice, such as a divider, a logo or a "to be continued" banner, in the break after each page, and optionally after the last. It's written into the strips themselves, so it scrolls seamlessly. The preview shows the first pages as readers will see them.

Vertical scrolling comics took off on Korean web platforms in the early 2000s, where they became known as webtoons. Instead of pages and spreads, the reader scrolls one long column, so pacing comes from space: a long gap can be a pause, and a reveal lands when it scrolls into view. Page-break art and gap size are your pacing tools.

Export for drawing

Plan and letter in inkflo, then keep drawing in Procreate, Photoshop, Affinity or Clip Studio Paint. Keep artwork writes a layered PSD of your art and groups; Drawing template writes the layout and lettering with empty Pencil, Ink and Colour layers in every panel. One page downloads a PSD; a range downloads a ZIP. Keep the .inkflo book for editable cuts, lettering and script.

Everywhere in inkflo

Copy and paste

Ctrl/⌘+C copies what you've selected; Ctrl/⌘+V pastes it under the pointer, on any page of any book.

  • Draw: pixels selected with Transform, lines selected with Select lines, or a placed image with its cut.
  • Caption: a selected balloon (with any joined balloons). Pasted lettering is unlinked from the script.
  • From other apps: paste a picture to place it as an image layer; paste text in Caption to make a balloon. Copied pictures can be pasted into other apps too.

Right-click and long press

Right-click the canvas, or hold a finger still on a tablet, for the actions that make sense where you are: switch tools, pick a colour, place a balloon of any kind, add a tail, cut tools, copy and paste, undo, fit the page, and export. Hover any button to see its name and shortcut.

Studio, Focus and Simple

Studio shows the tool bar, sidebar and desk tabs. Focus (Tab) gives the canvas the whole screen with floating controls that can hide themselves; Simple (Draw only) shows only the essential tools, which is good for beginners and small screens. Pinch to zoom and turn the canvas on a tablet; tap with two fingers to undo and three to redo.

For classrooms

  • Start from the starter comic. Students copy The Last Train, rewrite its two panels, then add a third: it teaches every desk in one lesson.
  • One desk per lesson. Script on day one, thumbnails and cuts on day two, pencils and inks, then lettering, then print. Each page's desk tabs show progress at a glance.
  • Studio roles. Swap .inkflo files between students so one writes, one draws and one letters, like a 1940s comic studio.
  • Zine project. Collage, a one-sheet mini-zine print, and a fold-and-staple class anthology using the booklet print mode.
  • Web comic. A vertical scroll episode with page-break art, uploaded as a simple web page.
  • Everything stays on the device. Nothing is uploaded; collect work as .inkflo files or exported PDFs.

Glossary

BeatOne moment in the script; usually one panel.
BleedArt printed past the trim so the cut page has no white edges.
ClosureThe reader filling in what happens between panels.
DownbeatThe panel that lands a page's punchline or turn, usually the last.
FlatsPlain colour fills laid down before shading.
G-penA flexible pen nib used widely in manga for lines that swell with pressure.
GutterThe space between panels.
InksThe final black lines drawn over pencils.
LetteringBalloons, captions and sound effects, and the craft of placing them.
PanelOne framed picture on the page.
Saddle stitchBinding with staples through the fold.
Safe areaThe inner part of the page where important things won't be trimmed.
ScreentoneA printed dot or line pattern used for shading.
Splash pageA page that is one big panel.
SpreadTwo facing pages seen together.
TailThe pointer from a balloon to its speaker.
TrimThe finished page size, where the paper is cut.
WebtoonA comic read by scrolling down one long column.
ZineA small self-published magazine, often photocopied and stapled.
